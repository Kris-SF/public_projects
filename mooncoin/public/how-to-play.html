Market Maker
by moontower

A market in a coin that does not exist. You and the people at the table set its price — and then four dice decide what it was worth.

Use → to move, Esc for the whole deck, S for notes. Four slides have sliders you can drag.

Twenty slides. Everything here is read straight out of the game's own code — the examples are not illustrations, they are the engine's actual output. Four slides are interactive.

The game

Three things, and that is the whole game

One coin
100

It opens at a price the host sets — 100 by default — and moves every round. There is nothing else to trade.

No money
∞

You never run out of cash and you never post margin. Buy as much as you dare. Nothing stops you but the price.

One score
P/L

Profit and loss is the only number kept. Every round it is recomputed from scratch against where the coin closed.

Because cash is infinite, size is free and only being wrong is expensive. That is deliberate — the game is about reading a market, not about managing a bank balance.

Stress the no-cash point early; new players spend the first round looking for a balance. There isn't one. P/L is mark-to-market only.

Shape of a round

Five beats, in this order, every round

1
The option auctionif switched on
You quote a price you would buy at and a price you would sell at. Everyone's quotes clear at once.
2
Stock orders
How many coins you want, and whether you want them now or only at today's price.
3
Cards
Everyone secretly plays cards that push the coin up, down, or wilder.
4
Four dice
Thrown one at a time. They decide which way the coin went and how far.
5
Settle
The coin lands on a new price. Everyone's P/L is restated against it.

Why options first? Because an option you were just handed is a risk you may want to offset by trading the coin itself — and the coin trades in beat 2, in the same round. Run stock first and every hedge arrives a round too late, after the dice have already settled the thing you were hedging.

Each beat is a gate: the round does not advance until everyone has confirmed or passed. The host can force a gate shut, and anyone who has not confirmed is simply left out — an un-confirmed order does not trade.

The one thing to get straight

Two prices, and they are not the same number

The print
103

Where you traded. Set in beat 2 by what the table asked for. If more people demanded coins than offered them, the print is above where the coin was.

The mark
124

Where it closed. Set in beat 4 by the dice. This is the number your P/L is measured against, and the number an option expiring today settles on.

Nothing links them. The print can be well above the mark, which means everyone who bought is down; or well below, which means everyone who bought is up. You choose the print by how hard you push. You do not choose the mark at all.

This is the single most common confusion. "I bought at 103 and the screen says 124 — where did 124 come from?" The dice came from. The print is the cost of your position; the mark is what the world decided it was worth.

Beat 2 · stock orders

Your orders make the print

Market order

"Get me filled, whatever it costs." A market order always fills, in full. You find out the price afterwards.

Limit order

"Only at today's price, not a penny worse." It sits there, and fills only if somebody needs what you are holding — and only as much as they needed.

If every buyer is matched by a seller the coin prints exactly where it was, no matter how much volume went through. And a limit order on the same side as the pressure does not trade at all — nobody is selling at yesterday's price into a rush of buyers.

The trade-off: a market order buys certainty and pays for it in price. A limit order is cheap and might not fill. When more limit orders are offered than the round needed, they are shared out pro-rata.

Compare: an imbalance of 1 moves it $1. 20 — twenty times as much — moves it only $5.

The move is the square root of the imbalance, rounded up — which is why the line climbs in steps rather than smoothly. So the first coin you demand is expensive and the fiftieth is nearly free. Size is cheap in this market — which is exactly why being on the wrong side of the dice with a lot of it is what ruins a round.

Real markets behave roughly this way and it is the single most useful intuition to take away: impact is concave. The flip side — if you are the one providing the resting offers, you are getting paid very little for a lot of risk.

Beat 3 · cards

Four kinds of card, played face down

↑
Up
Leans the coin upward. Pushes the trend die.
↓
Down
Leans the coin downward. Pushes the trend die the other way.
×
Multiply
Makes the move bigger. Pushes the volatility die.
+
Add
Makes the move smaller. Pushes the volatility die the other way.

Only the net matters. Three ↑ against one ↓ counts as two ↑. And the net is capped by the dice: three net cards one way settles the direction outright — a fourth buys you nothing and is spent all the same. Everyone plays at once, in secret, so you are guessing at the table as much as at the dice.

Cards are a finite hand dealt at the start — ten each by default, drawn from an even deck. Spending three ↑ to guarantee an up round is a real option, and an expensive one. Nobody sees what anyone played until the gate shuts.

Every round, from scratch:
(where it closed − what you paid) × how many you hold.

Short works the same way with the sign flipped — sell 6 at the same print and you are down 126. There is no cash account, no interest, no fees. The only thing that moves your score is the gap between your price and the closing price.

Positions carry across rounds, so P/L is recomputed against each new mark, not banked. Somebody can be up 200 in round 3 and flat by round 5 without ever trading again.

The optional second layer

Options

Switched on at setup. The stock-only game is exactly as it was — this adds a market in bets on where the coin ends up, and makes you the one quoting the prices.

If the table is new, play a full game without this first. With options on, every round gains the auction beat — one gate per expiry — which roughly doubles the length of a round.

Options · what they are

Two kinds of bet, and a price you pay for it

call

A bet the coin finishes above a number. Buy the $100 call and if the coin closes at $124 it pays you the $24 it finished above 100. Close below 100 and it pays nothing.

put

A bet the coin finishes below a number. Buy the $100 put and a close at $94 pays you the $6 it finished below. Close above and it pays nothing.

The strike

The number in the bet — $100 above. The game lists nine of them and you pick which to trade.

The premium

What you pay for the bet. Nobody tells you this number. The table discovers it.

Nothing in this game ever quotes an option for you. There is no model, no suggested value, no fair price on the screen. The only option price that exists is one the table agreed on — which is the entire exercise.

Resist the urge to teach Black-Scholes. The point is that a price comes out of a market, and a price that comes out of a market can be wrong. An option struck for the current round is a pure bet on one dice roll.

  • Calls read down the left, puts down the right, strikes up the middle — the layout a real trading screen uses.
  • The shaded middle row is at the money: the strike sitting exactly where the coin is, $100 here. Four strikes either side, $5 apart.
  • The board re-centres every round. If the coin closes at 112, next round's strikes run 92 to 132 and the old ones are gone.
  • Your position keeps its strike forever. A 100 call you bought settles against 100 even after that strike has drifted off the board.
The anchor is not rounded — it is wherever the coin closed, so the strikes are only round numbers by luck. That keeps the middle row exactly at the money rather than near it.

Options · the auction

You quote both sides into a question mark

  • The board shows which strikes carry an order — and nothing else. Where the orders landed is itself a clue.
  • Side hidden  shown as ? — you do not know whether the house is buying or selling. You must quote a price both ways.
  • House buys  shown as B? — sometimes it tells you the direction up front. Shade accordingly.
  • Size is always hidden. Even a telegraphed order might be for one contract or for ten.

Quoting a market is not the same as filling an order. You have to name a price you would stand behind in either direction.

Everybody quotes at once, in secret, and the gate stays shut until every player has confirmed or passed. Then the orders are revealed and everything clears together. A quote you typed but never confirmed does not trade — not even if the host forces the gate.

The house order's side and size genuinely do not exist until the gate closes — they are generated at that moment, so there is nothing to leak. Which means quoting wide "just in case" is safe and expensive, and quoting tight is where the game is.

  • Your bid is the most you would pay. Green, because green is what you make money on.
  • Your ask is the least you would accept. Red.
  • Sizes sit outside the prices, so a two-sided market reads out from its own spread — exactly like the board.
  • Leave a side blank to quote one way only. Leave everything blank and confirm to pass.

Only strikes carrying a house order are listed, because only those can clear. A quote anywhere else could never trade.

Field order matters and is deliberate: bid size, bid, ask, ask size. If somebody fills in the prices back to front the quote is crossed and the table will take both sides off them.

Ben asked 18 and sold at 12. Your bid of 20 crossed his offer, and the clearing price governs every trade in the contract — so you paid 12 for something you had said you would pay 20 for, and he took 12 for something he had asked 18 for. Offering behind the best price is not safe. It is a resting order at a price you never named.

Two steps, always in this order: the house order takes what it needs first, and the price it first trades at becomes the clearing price. Then any leftover crossing interest among the players trades at that same price.

Between players, everything prints at the clearing price. Ben's 11 was never honoured, and your 14 was never charged.

And if nobody quotes the side the house needs, the contract simply does not trade. No fill, no price, no robot stepping in to make one. A strike everybody passed on stays blank all round.

Yes, these two rules point different directions, and that is the real asymmetry to understand: the house pays up as it walks, players get dragged to the clearing price. Showing size behind the best price is the expensive habit.

Settlement is arithmetic, not opinion: what it finished above the strike, less what you paid.

Three places to stop the slider: a close at 124 makes the call worth 24 against the 12 you paid, so +12; a close at 112 is worth exactly what you paid, so 0; a close at 94 is worth nothing, so -12 — and never worse than that. Flip to selling and every number is mirrored: the seller's best round is +12 and their worst has no floor at all.

The asymmetry is the lesson. A buyer's loss is capped at the premium; a seller's is not. Options expiring in the current round settle against the mark the dice just set — not against the print.

Go

Six things worth knowing on your first game

  • Confirm, or you are not in. Typed is not traded. Every beat is a gate and it can shut without you.
  • Passing is a move. No order, no quote, no cards — perfectly legal, and often right.
  • Size is cheap, being wrong is not. The print barely moves on big orders. The mark does not care how big you are.
  • Watch what the table did. The order book is shown after every round. Who bought, how much, at what.
  • Quote wider than feels clever. You are naming a price in both directions against an unknown. Once is enough to learn this.
  • Short options have no ceiling. It is the one position in the game that can lose more than you expected to make.

The whole game is one question, asked five times:
what is this worth, and is the table wrong about it?

Default game is five rounds, ten cards each, opening at 100. All three are host-settable, along with whether options are on and which rounds carry an expiry. Shorter first game: three rounds, options off.
